Multiple Perspectives

Seven Viewpoints on Watergate

Watergate looked completely different depending on where you stood. This page covers seven distinct perspectives, from inside the White House to the Senate hearing room to living rooms across the country.

Thesis Connection

The revolutionary abuse of power at Watergate's core produced a reaction that came from every direction at once. Understanding why that reaction was so powerful also means taking seriously the perspective of those who initially resisted it. Every perspective below circles back to the same question: what happens when a president puts himself above the law?

Why Perspective Matters

Seeing It From Different Angles

History is not one story. To understand Watergate honestly, I needed to look at it from multiple angles, including the people who were wrong and the people who were complicated. For most of 1972 and 1973, public opinion was genuinely divided. Millions of Americans believed Nixon was the victim of a political witch hunt. Understanding why is part of understanding the story.

RN

Richard Nixon and the White House

The Perspective of the Accused

Nixon's public position was consistent for two years: he knew nothing about the break-in, his White House had investigated itself and found no wrongdoing, and the whole investigation was a politically motivated attack by the liberal media. He stuck to that story until the smoking gun tape made it completely untenable.

Privately, the tapes show a more complicated picture. Nixon genuinely believed that previous administrations had done many of the same things without criminal scrutiny. Kennedy authorized FBI surveillance of civil rights leaders. Johnson wiretapped political opponents. Nixon's complaint that he was being held to a different standard was not entirely fabricated, though it does not justify the cover-up. What Nixon never accepted was that obstructing justice and paying hush money to witnesses was categorically different from ordinary political hardball. His resignation speech never acknowledged guilt.

"I am not a crook. I've earned everything I've got."
Richard Nixon, press conference, Orlando, Florida, November 17, 1973
W+B

Woodward, Bernstein, and the Washington Post

The Investigative Journalism Perspective

In June 1972, Bob Woodward was 29 and had been at the Washington Post for nine months. Carl Bernstein was 28. Neither was famous. They were assigned to cover what most of Washington dismissed as a minor burglary. Within weeks, they had connected the burglars to CREEP and to senior White House officials, working with a secret FBI source later revealed as Associate Director Mark Felt.

From their perspective, the reporting was never about getting Nixon. It was about following the evidence wherever it led. The White House tried hard to discredit the Post, including threatening its broadcast licenses. Woodward and Bernstein demonstrated that investigative journalism, done carefully, is a real democratic accountability mechanism. Their work inspired a generation of reporters.

"We were not out to get Nixon. We were out to get the story. They turned out to be the same thing."
Carl Bernstein, in various interviews
SE

Congress: Ervin and Rodino

The Legislative Oversight Perspective

Senator Sam Ervin of North Carolina, who chaired the Senate Watergate Committee, was 76 years old and a self-described "country lawyer" who quoted the Constitution from memory. His conservative credentials made it impossible to dismiss the hearings as a liberal exercise. From Congress's perspective, Watergate was a direct challenge to the separation of powers. Nixon had treated Congress as an obstacle, impounding funds, conducting secret wars, and claiming executive privilege over everything.

Republican Senator Howard Baker's question became the defining line of the crisis: "What did the president know, and when did he know it?" The fact that a Nixon-allied Republican asked it shows how serious the constitutional stakes were. In the House, Judiciary Committee Chairman Peter Rodino ran the impeachment inquiry with genuine fairness, and the bipartisan votes for impeachment gave the process legitimacy that a purely partisan effort could not have had.

"What did the president know, and when did he know it?"
Senator Howard Baker (R-TN), Senate Watergate Committee, 1973
JS

The Judiciary: Sirica and the Supreme Court

The Rule-of-Law Perspective

Judge John Sirica is probably the most underrated figure in the entire story. A Nixon appointee, he presided over the original burglars' trial and sensed the full story was not coming out. His strategy of imposing stiff provisional sentences and inviting defendants to cooperate was aggressive. Some civil libertarians criticized it. But it worked: it is largely why McCord wrote his letter and why the cover-up cracked open in early 1973.

The Supreme Court's unanimous ruling in United States v. Nixon was the most constitutionally significant moment of the whole crisis. All eight participating justices, including Nixon's four appointees, held that executive privilege is real but not absolute. It cannot shield criminal evidence from a legitimate prosecution. The 8 to 0 vote was deliberately unambiguous.

"Neither the doctrine of separation of powers, nor the need for confidentiality, can sustain an absolute, unqualified presidential privilege of immunity from judicial process."
Chief Justice Warren Burger, United States v. Nixon, 418 U.S. 683, 706 (1974)
USA

The American Public

The Citizen Perspective

The public's reaction to Watergate was slow, then overwhelming. In November 1972, Americans had just given Nixon one of the greatest landslides in history. A year later, his approval rating was 27%. That shift required months of televised hearings, specific dramatic moments like the taping system revelation and the Saturday Night Massacre, and finally the release of the smoking gun tape.

Gallup polling shows that public trust in the federal government to do what is right dropped from 53% in 1972 to 36% in 1974. That collapse was rational. People had just watched their president lie to them for two years. But the resulting cynicism has been one of Watergate's most durable and damaging legacies, making it harder to distinguish real corruption from ordinary political disagreement.

DEF

Nixon's Defenders

The Other Side

I think it is important to take Nixon's defenders seriously rather than just dismissing them. Millions of real people genuinely believed Watergate was a political hit job. Their strongest arguments: the Washington Post had endorsed McGovern, the Senate committee was controlled by Democrats, and previous presidents had authorized wiretaps and FBI surveillance without facing criminal scrutiny. None of these arguments are entirely wrong on the facts.

Where defenders ultimately collapsed was on the facts. The smoking gun tape of June 23, 1972 showed Nixon personally ordering obstruction of justice. Representative Charles Wiggins, who had argued most passionately against impeachment, announced after reading the transcript that he could no longer defend the president. Facts answered the arguments definitively.

WIT

The Whistleblowers and Key Witnesses

The Insider Perspective

The people who broke the cover-up from the inside are the most morally complicated figures in the story. John Dean, Mark Felt, Alexander Butterfield, and James McCord all participated in the cover-up to some degree, and their decisions to cooperate were driven by a mix of principle and self-interest.

Dean began cooperating in April 1973, partly because he recognized the administration was positioning him to take the fall. Felt leaked to Woodward and Bernstein because he was troubled by Nixon's interference with the FBI, and also because he had been passed over for FBI director. Butterfield, when asked directly about a taping system, simply told the truth. Together, these witnesses illustrate why the cover-up eventually failed: it required too many people to stay silent, and people have their own interests and their own consciences.

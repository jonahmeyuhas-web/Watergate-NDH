Multiple Perspectives

Seven Viewpoints on Watergate

Watergate was not one story — it was seven. The people who lived through it experienced it radically differently depending on where they stood: in the Oval Office, in a newsroom, in the Senate hearing room, on a jury, or at home watching television. A complete historical understanding requires engaging with all of these perspectives honestly and critically.

Thesis Connection

The revolutionary abuse of power at Watergate's core produced a reaction that came from every direction simultaneously — the press, Congress, the courts, and the public. But understanding why that reaction was so strong requires also understanding the perspective of those who initially resisted it: Nixon's defenders, who were not simply dishonest people, but men and women who genuinely believed they were protecting the country from political persecution. Every perspective below ultimately circles back to the same question: how should a democracy respond when its leader betrays its trust?

A Note on Perspective

Why Multiple Viewpoints Matter

History is not a single narrative. It is a collision of experiences, beliefs, and interpretations — and honest historical scholarship requires taking seriously the perspectives of people whose views we might find uncomfortable or even wrong. This does not mean treating all perspectives as equally valid. Nixon ordered obstruction of justice; that is not a matter of "perspective," it is a documented fact. But understanding why Nixon and his defenders believed what they believed — and why millions of Americans initially agreed with them — is essential to understanding how Watergate happened and what it revealed about American political culture.

What follows are seven distinct perspectives on the Watergate crisis, presented as honestly as the historical record allows. Where perspectives are self-serving or factually mistaken, I say so — but I try first to understand them on their own terms.

🎭

Richard Nixon and the White House

The Perspective of the Accused

Richard Nixon's perspective on Watergate evolved — and was strategically constructed — over time. In the immediate aftermath of the break-in, Nixon's public position was one of innocence: he had known nothing about the break-in, his White House had investigated itself and found no wrongdoing, and the whole affair was a partisan witch hunt being prosecuted by his media enemies, particularly the liberal Washington Post. He maintained this public position for more than two years.

Privately, Nixon's thinking was more complex and, paradoxically, more revealing of his worldview. The White House tapes show a man who genuinely believed that many of the things he had done — the wiretapping of journalists and his own staff, the use of the IRS against enemies, the secret bombing of Cambodia — were justified by national security. Nixon operated within a Cold War framework that held that the president had not merely the right but the duty to take extraordinary measures to protect the country from its enemies, foreign and domestic.

Nixon also believed, sincerely and with some historical basis, that previous administrations had done many of the same things without facing criminal scrutiny. The Kennedy and Johnson administrations had authorized illegal wiretaps of civil rights leaders and journalists. The FBI under J. Edgar Hoover had run systematic programs of illegal surveillance and harassment for decades. Nixon's complaint that he was being held to a standard his predecessors had never faced was not entirely without merit — though it is not a legal or moral defense of his conduct.

"I am not a crook. I've earned everything I've got."
Richard Nixon, press conference, Orlando, Florida, November 17, 1973

What Nixon never fully accepted — or at least never publicly acknowledged — was that the cover-up was fundamentally different from the underlying crime. The burglary of the Watergate complex was a serious felony. But the cover-up — directing the CIA to obstruct the FBI, paying hush money to witnesses, lying to the public — was an assault on the constitutional order itself. Nixon's defenders were correct that previous administrations had committed abuses. They were wrong to argue that this meant Nixon's abuses were acceptable.

Nixon's resignation speech on August 8, 1974, is a masterpiece of evasion. He framed his departure as a political sacrifice — he was resigning because he had lost his congressional base, not because he had committed crimes. "I have always tried to do what is best for the Nation," he said. He did not apologize. He did not admit guilt. Even at the end, Nixon could not acknowledge what the rest of the country had concluded: that he had betrayed the trust the American people had placed in him.

Woodward, Bernstein, and the Washington Post

The Investigative Journalism Perspective

Bob Woodward and Carl Bernstein were not, in June 1972, celebrated reporters. Woodward was 29 and had been at the Post for nine months. Bernstein was 28. They were assigned to cover what most of Washington initially dismissed as a minor burglary story. Within weeks, they had concluded — and were able to document — that the Watergate break-in was part of a systematic campaign of political espionage and sabotage that ran directly from CREEP to the White House itself.

What made Woodward and Bernstein's reporting genuinely revolutionary for American journalism was not just that they got the story — it was how they got it. Working with a secret source known as "Deep Throat" (later revealed in 2005 as FBI Associate Director W. Mark Felt), they built their reporting through hundreds of interviews, careful document analysis, and a commitment to two-source verification that was rigorous even by the standards of that era. The Post's executive editor Ben Bradlee and publisher Katharine Graham deserve enormous credit for backing the reporters when the Nixon White House mounted a furious campaign to discredit the paper and threatened the broadcast licenses of Post-owned television stations.

From the reporters' perspective, what they were doing was not political — it was journalistic. They followed the evidence where it led. That it led to the White House was not their choice. Their obligation, as they understood it, was to the public's right to know what its government was doing in its name.

"We were not out to get Nixon. We were out to get the story. They turned out to be the same thing."
Carl Bernstein, interview, various sources

From a historical perspective, what Woodward and Bernstein demonstrated was that a free press, properly resourced and editorially courageous, is an irreplaceable safeguard of democracy. The White House's attempts to destroy the Post's credibility — calling their reporting "political garbage," pressuring advertisers, threatening broadcast licenses — were not successful. And the reason they were not successful is that the reporters' work was accurate. When the tapes were released, they confirmed what the Post had been reporting for two years.

The lasting significance of the Woodward-Bernstein model for investigative journalism is enormous. Their work inspired a generation of reporters and established that the press's adversarial relationship with the executive branch was not a partisan exercise — it was a constitutional function. As Woodward and Bernstein documented in their 1974 book All the President's Men, the story of how they broke Watergate is also the story of how a free press operates in a democracy: imperfectly, sometimes recklessly, but ultimately in service of the public's right to know.

Congress: The Ervin Committee and the Judiciary Committee

The Legislative Oversight Perspective

Senator Sam Ervin of North Carolina was 76 years old when he chaired the Senate Watergate Committee. A self-described "country lawyer" who could quote the Bible and the Constitution with equal facility, Ervin was in many ways the perfect choice to lead the investigation: his conservative credentials made it impossible to dismiss the hearings as a liberal partisan exercise, and his genuine reverence for constitutional principles gave the investigation a moral seriousness that transcended partisan politics.

From the congressional perspective, Watergate represented a fundamental challenge to the separation of powers. The Nixon administration had treated Congress as an obstacle to be managed rather than a co-equal branch of government. Nixon impounded funds Congress had appropriated, conducted secret wars without congressional authorization, and claimed executive privilege so broadly that it would have made congressional oversight of the executive essentially impossible. The Senate Watergate Committee's investigation was not merely about a break-in — it was about whether Congress could exercise its constitutional responsibility to hold the executive branch accountable.

Republican Senator Howard Baker captured the committee's central question in a phrase that became one of the most famous in American political history: "What did the president know, and when did he know it?" Baker asked this question not as Nixon's enemy, but as a Republican senator genuinely trying to determine the facts. That a member of Nixon's own party posed the definitive question of the investigation reflects how serious the constitutional concerns were.

"What did the president know, and when did he know it?"
Senator Howard Baker (R-TN), Senate Watergate Committee hearings, 1973

In the House, Judiciary Committee Chairman Peter Rodino of New Jersey managed the impeachment process with notable fairness. He insisted on a bipartisan process, allowed Nixon's lawyers to participate in the inquiry, and encouraged committee members to follow the evidence rather than their partisan instincts. The result was a committee that voted for three articles of impeachment with bipartisan support — a remarkable achievement given how politically charged the moment was.

From the congressional perspective, Watergate was ultimately a success story: the legislative branch performed its constitutional function under enormous pressure and forced executive accountability. But it also revealed how dependent that function is on individual courage. Had the Ervin Committee been chaired by a more partisan or less constitutionally serious figure, or had the House Judiciary Committee operated more as a partisan instrument, the outcome might have been very different.

⚖️

The Judiciary: Sirica and the Supreme Court

The Rule-of-Law Perspective

Chief Judge John Sirica of the U.S. District Court for the District of Columbia was perhaps the most important figure in the early stages of Watergate — and also one of the least celebrated. A Nixon appointee, Sirica presided over the original trial of the Watergate burglars. Sensing that the full story was being concealed, he imposed stiff provisional sentences — up to 40 years for McCord — and explicitly invited defendants to cooperate. His strategy worked: McCord wrote his famous letter to Sirica in March 1973, cracking the cover-up.

Sirica was criticized by some civil libertarians for his aggressive judicial approach — his provisional sentencing was seen by some as judicial coercion, using the threat of prison to compel testimony. From Sirica's perspective, he was doing what the evidence demanded: pressing for truth in a case where it was obvious that the whole story had not been told.

The Supreme Court's perspective on Watergate, expressed in the unanimous July 24, 1974, ruling in United States v. Nixon, was the most legally significant of any institution involved. Writing for a unanimous Court, Chief Justice Warren Burger — a Nixon appointee — held that executive privilege, while real, is not absolute. The president's generalized interest in confidentiality cannot override the specific evidentiary needs of a criminal trial.

"Neither the doctrine of separation of powers, nor the need for confidentiality of high-level communications, without more, can sustain an absolute, unqualified presidential privilege of immunity from judicial process under all circumstances."
Chief Justice Warren Burger, United States v. Nixon, 418 U.S. 683, 706 (1974)

The Court's ruling established one of the most important constitutional principles in American history: no person, including the president, is above the law or exempt from the obligation to produce evidence in a criminal proceeding. The 8–0 vote — not a single dissent, even from Nixon's four appointees — was deliberately designed to leave Nixon no room to maneuver. The Court understood that a fractured or close ruling might have given Nixon political cover to defy it. The unanimous ruling did not.

From the judiciary's perspective, Watergate was a moment of institutional validation: the courts had been tested and had held. But it was also a reminder of the system's vulnerability. Judicial authority ultimately depends on executive compliance — Nixon could have defied the ruling. That he did not may reflect his political judgment rather than principled respect for the rule of law.

The American Public

The Citizen Perspective

The American public's reaction to Watergate was neither immediate nor uniform — and that gradual, contested shift in public opinion is one of the most historically interesting aspects of the whole affair. In November 1972, Americans had just given Nixon one of the greatest electoral landslides in history. A year later, his approval rating had fallen to 27%. Understanding that shift requires understanding what the public was seeing and how they were interpreting it.

In the early months of 1973, public opinion was genuinely divided. Many Americans, particularly Nixon's core supporters in the South and Midwest, believed what the White House was telling them: that Watergate was a minor story being blown out of proportion by Nixon's enemies in the liberal media. The Washington Post, in this view, was pursuing a political vendetta against a president it had opposed in two elections. The Senate Watergate Committee hearings were a partisan exercise by Democrats who had never accepted Nixon's presidency.

What changed minds was the televised hearings themselves. Beginning in May 1973, Americans sat glued to their television sets as John Dean, Alexander Butterfield, and a parade of other witnesses described — in specific, documentary detail — a criminal conspiracy that had operated out of the White House. The revelation of the taping system electrified the country. The Saturday Night Massacre — Nixon's firing of Special Prosecutor Cox — produced such a visceral negative reaction that calls to the Capitol Hill switchboard temporarily overwhelmed the system.

"The people who did Watergate and who did their best to destroy it — none of them went to jail without a fight. The people who worked to expose it — people like John Dean who risked everything — they were ultimately vindicated by the facts."
Public sentiment widely documented in contemporaneous Gallup polling, 1973–1974

Gallup polling data shows the arc clearly: public trust in the federal government to "do what is right" most or all of the time fell from 53% in 1972 to 36% in 1974 — a collapse that would continue for decades. Watergate did not create American cynicism about government; Vietnam had already planted those seeds. But Watergate watered them, and the resulting cynicism has become one of the most durable features of American political culture.

For ordinary Americans watching the hearings in the summer of 1973, Watergate was not an abstraction — it was a betrayal. The constitutional principle at stake — that the president should be subject to the same laws as everyone else — resonated deeply with Americans whose entire political tradition is built on the rejection of monarchy and the rule of a single person above the law. Nixon's claimed right to withhold evidence from courts and Congress struck many Americans not as a legitimate constitutional position but as something that sounded uncomfortably like the kings they had fought a revolution to escape.

🛡️

Nixon's Defenders

The Loyal Opposition's Perspective

It would be historically incomplete — and intellectually dishonest — to present Watergate as a story in which everyone immediately recognized Nixon's guilt and demanded accountability. Millions of Americans, including prominent politicians, supported Nixon throughout much of the crisis and believed, sincerely, that the investigation was a politically motivated attack on a legitimately elected president.

Nixon's defenders made several arguments that deserve serious engagement. First: the political-persecution argument. Nixon's most ardent supporters pointed out that the Washington Post, which was driving the story, had endorsed McGovern in 1972 and had been a consistent Nixon critic. The Senate Watergate Committee was controlled by Democrats. The entire investigation, they argued, was a continuation of the political warfare that had been waged against Nixon since his days as a Communist-hunting congressman. That powerful people hated Nixon was a fact; the question was whether their hatred had motivated an investigation or merely an exposure of legitimate wrongdoing.

Second: the "everyone does it" argument. Nixon's defenders, with some historical accuracy, noted that previous administrations had engaged in many of the same practices Nixon was being investigated for. The Kennedy administration had used the FBI against political opponents. The Johnson administration had wiretapped political opponents and civil rights leaders. J. Edgar Hoover's FBI had conducted decades of illegal surveillance. Why, Nixon's defenders asked, was Nixon being held to a standard that had never been applied to his predecessors?

"He could be so good, and he was so bad. He was a political genius who somehow couldn't resist doing self-destructive things."
Pat Buchanan, Nixon speechwriter and defender, reflecting on Nixon years later

Third: the national security argument. Some defenders argued that the measures Nixon took — surveillance of dissidents, the Plumbers unit, the cover-up itself — were motivated by genuine national security concerns in an era of Cold War tension, domestic radicalism, and a bitter, ongoing war. From this perspective, Nixon was not a corrupt politician covering up crimes for personal gain; he was a president who had made hard choices in difficult circumstances and was being destroyed for it.

Where did the defenders ultimately go wrong? On the facts. The "smoking gun" tape of June 23, 1972, proved beyond any reasonable doubt that Nixon had personally ordered obstruction of justice within six days of the break-in. This was not a matter of political interpretation or disputed evidence. Even Nixon's most loyal congressional defenders — including Representative Charles Wiggins of California, who had argued passionately against the articles of impeachment — announced they could no longer support the president after the June 23 tape was released. The facts, in the end, answered the defenders' arguments. But the fact that credible people made those arguments for two years is itself an important historical truth about how divided the country was.

🗣️

Whistleblowers and Key Witnesses

The Insider Perspective

The most fascinating — and in some ways most morally complex — perspective on Watergate belongs to the men who were inside the Nixon administration, participated in its crimes, and then chose to tell the truth. These are not heroes in any simple sense: John Dean, Mark Felt, Alexander Butterfield, and others who cooperated with investigators did so for complicated reasons that mixed genuine principle with self-interest and self-preservation. Understanding their perspectives requires holding both realities simultaneously.

John Dean was Nixon's White House Counsel and one of the architects of the cover-up. Dean participated in obstruction of justice, coordinated hush money payments, and lied to investigators for months. He began cooperating with prosecutors in April 1973 — after it became clear that the administration was preparing to make him the scapegoat. Dean's testimony was devastating to Nixon, precisely because he had been inside the conspiracy. His decision to testify was motivated partly by genuine revulsion at what he had participated in, and partly by the entirely self-interested calculation that cooperation offered him a better outcome than continued silence. Dean's complex motivations don't invalidate his testimony — the tapes ultimately confirmed virtually everything he said — but they complicate any simple heroic narrative.

Mark Felt — "Deep Throat" for three decades — was the FBI's Associate Director, second only to J. Edgar Hoover's successor, L. Patrick Gray. Felt leaked information to Woodward and Bernstein not purely out of public-spirited outrage but also because he was furious that Nixon had passed him over for FBI director after Hoover's death. Felt's personal grievance and his genuine belief that Nixon was corrupting the FBI are not mutually exclusive — but understanding that his whistleblowing was partly motivated by institutional loyalty and personal ambition, not only principle, gives a more accurate picture of how the cover-up was dismantled.

"I know every one of those eight words [on the smoking gun tape] by heart — 'Pay money. It would be wrong.' Those words finished him."
John Dean, reflecting on the "smoking gun" tape in various interviews

Alexander Butterfield — the White House aide who revealed the taping system — appears to have acted primarily out of a straightforward commitment to truthfulness. When Senate investigators, acting on a tip, asked Butterfield directly whether there was a White House taping system, he confirmed it. He later said he believed the truth had to come out. His revelation was the single most important turning point in the investigation.

Together, these witnesses illustrate something important about how the revolutionary abuse of Nixon's cover-up ultimately failed: it required the silence of too many people with too many different interests. A cover-up is only as strong as its weakest link, and a cover-up involving dozens of senior government officials, each with their own ambitions and legal exposures, was structurally fragile. The reaction against Nixon's abuse came not just from outside institutions — Congress, courts, the press — but from within his own circle, from men who had participated in the crimes and ultimately decided that their own survival, or their own conscience, required them to tell the truth.

Synthesis: What These Seven Perspectives Tell Us

What strikes me most, looking at all seven perspectives together, is how differently people can see the same events — and how the passage of time and the revelation of new evidence can transform those views. In 1972, millions of Americans were convinced that Watergate was either a minor story or a political witch hunt. By August 1974, with the smoking gun tape released, even Nixon's most loyal defenders abandoned him.

This transformation is itself historically significant. It suggests that what ultimately defeated Nixon was not public opinion or partisan politics — it was evidence. The White House taping system, installed to serve Nixon's own purposes, became the instrument of his destruction because it produced incontrovertible evidence of his guilt. Against documented evidence, even the most politically motivated defenses eventually collapsed.

The perspectives of whistleblowers and witnesses also reveal something important about how institutional cultures change. The men who broke the cover-up — Dean, Felt, Butterfield, McCord — did so for different reasons, at different times, from different positions. But they all ultimately chose truth over silence. Whether out of self-preservation, principle, or both, their decisions illustrate the essential insight of Watergate as a reform story: institutions are ultimately composed of individuals, and individuals make choices. The reaction against Nixon's revolutionary abuse was made possible by enough individuals — in courts, in newsrooms, in Senate chambers, and even within the White House itself — choosing to make the right choice.

The Watergate Scandal

How a Third-Rate Burglary Brought Down a President

By Jonah Meyuhas and Matis Gelinas

Thesis

The Watergate scandal was a revolutionary abuse of presidential power. Nixon used the FBI, CIA, and Justice Department against his own political opponents. That abuse provoked a national reaction from journalists, courts, and Congress, and ultimately drove reforms that permanently changed how American democracy holds its president accountable.

Introduction

Why Watergate Still Matters

On June 17, 1972, a security guard named Frank Wills noticed tape on a door latch at the Watergate office complex. He called the police. Five men were arrested inside the Democratic National Committee headquarters, and that small discovery started one of the biggest political crises in American history.

But Watergate was not just about the break-in. Nixon used the CIA to block the FBI's investigation, paid hush money to keep witnesses quiet, and lied to the public for over two years. He resigned on August 9, 1974, the only American president ever to do so.

I chose Watergate because it fits the NHD theme better than almost any other event. The revolution is Nixon's abuse of power. The reaction is what journalists, courts, and Congress did about it. The reform is the wave of laws that followed. All three are clear, dramatic, and consequential. What makes the story especially strange is that Nixon did not need to cheat. He won 49 states in 1972. The crimes that ended his presidency were completely unnecessary.

Quick Facts

  • Break-in: June 17, 1972 at the Watergate complex
  • Resignation: August 8, 1974, Richard Nixon
  • Duration: 26 months from break-in to resignation
  • Key ruling: United States v. Nixon (1974), 8 to 0
  • Convictions: 69 indicted, 48 convicted or pleaded guilty
  • First: Only presidential resignation in U.S. history
Theme Connection

Revolution, Reaction, Reform

Revolution

The Abuse of Power

Nixon tried to turn the federal government into a weapon against his political enemies. That is the revolutionary act at Watergate's center. Not the burglary itself, but the systematic effort to place the presidency above the law.

Read more

Reaction

The Pushback

The press, Congress, the courts, and ultimately the public all pushed back. The Saturday Night Massacre alone produced 300,000 telegrams to Congress in two days. The reaction came from every direction at once.

Read more

Reform

The Laws That Followed

Congress responded with real structural change: campaign finance limits, ethics disclosure requirements, FISA courts, the Presidential Records Act, and the independent counsel statute. These reforms still shape American government today.

Key People

Who Was Involved

RN

Richard Nixon

37th President of the United States

Nixon ordered the CIA to obstruct the FBI's investigation six days after the break-in. That conversation, recorded on tape, ended his presidency when it was finally released in August 1974.

W+B

Woodward and Bernstein

Washington Post Reporters

Two young reporters who broke and sustained the story, working with a secret FBI source called Deep Throat. Their work kept Watergate in the public eye when the White House was trying to make it disappear.

JD

John Dean

White House Counsel, turned state's witness

One of the architects of the cover-up, Dean eventually cooperated with prosecutors. His Senate testimony placed Nixon at the center of the conspiracy, and the tapes later confirmed almost every detail.

JS

Judge John Sirica

Chief Judge, U.S. District Court, D.C.

Presided over the burglars' trial and pressed for the full story. His strategy of imposing stiff provisional sentences to pressure cooperation is largely why the cover-up cracked open in early 1973.

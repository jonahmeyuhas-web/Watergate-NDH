Watergate: Revolution, Reaction, Reform

How a Third-Rate Burglary Brought Down a President

Thesis

The Watergate scandal was a revolutionary abuse of presidential power — Nixon used the FBI, CIA, and Justice Department against his own political opponents — that provoked a national reaction from journalists, courts, and Congress, ultimately driving reforms that permanently changed how American democracy holds its president accountable.

Introduction

Why Watergate Still Matters

On June 17, 1972, a security guard named Frank Wills noticed tape on a door latch at the Watergate office complex in Washington D.C. He called the police. Five men were arrested inside the Democratic National Committee headquarters. That small discovery started one of the biggest political crises in American history.

But Watergate wasn't just a break-in — it was a cover-up ordered from the Oval Office. Nixon used the CIA to block the FBI's investigation, paid hush money to keep witnesses quiet, and lied to the public for over two years. He resigned on August 9, 1974, the only American president ever to do so.

I chose Watergate for this project because it fits the NHD theme better than almost any other event I could think of. The revolution is Nixon's abuse of presidential power. The reaction is what journalists, courts, and Congress did about it. The reform is the wave of laws that followed — laws that still shape American government today. All three are clear, dramatic, and historically significant.

What's strange about Watergate is that Nixon didn't need to cheat. He won 49 states in 1972. He was going to win by a landslide no matter what. The crimes that destroyed his presidency were completely unnecessary — which is part of what makes this story so fascinating.

Quick Facts

  • Break-in: June 17, 1972 at the Watergate complex
  • Resignation: August 8, 1974 — Richard Nixon
  • Duration: 26 months from break-in to resignation
  • Key ruling: United States v. Nixon (1974) — 8–0
  • Convictions: 69 indicted, 48 convicted or pleaded guilty
  • First: Only presidential resignation in U.S. history
Theme Connection

Revolution, Reaction, Reform — All Three Are Here

Revolution

The Abuse of Power

Nixon tried to turn the federal government into a weapon against his political enemies. That's the revolutionary act at the center of Watergate — not the burglary, but the systematic attempt to place the presidency above the law.

Read more

Reaction

The Pushback

The press, Congress, the courts, and ultimately the public all reacted — each in different ways, at different times. The Saturday Night Massacre alone produced 300,000 telegrams to Congress in two days. The reaction was overwhelming.

Read more

Reform

The Laws That Followed

Congress responded with real structural change: campaign finance limits, ethics disclosure requirements, FISA courts, the Presidential Records Act, and the independent counsel statute. These reforms still shape American government.

Key People

Who Was Involved

RN

Richard Nixon

37th President of the United States

The central figure. Nixon ordered the CIA to obstruct the FBI's investigation just six days after the break-in. The tape recording of that conversation ended his presidency when it was finally released in August 1974.

W+B

Woodward & Bernstein

Washington Post Reporters

Two young reporters who broke and sustained the story, working with a secret FBI source called "Deep Throat" (later revealed as FBI Associate Director Mark Felt). Their work kept Watergate in the public eye when the White House was trying to make it disappear.

JD

John Dean

White House Counsel — Turned State's Witness

Nixon's lawyer, and one of the architects of the cover-up. Dean eventually decided to cooperate with prosecutors. His Senate testimony in June 1973 placed Nixon at the center of the conspiracy — and was later confirmed in almost every detail by the tapes.

JS

Judge John Sirica

Chief Judge, U.S. District Court, D.C.

Presided over the burglars' trial and imposed stiff provisional sentences to pressure defendants to talk. His strategy worked — it's largely how the cover-up cracked open in the first place.

Annotated Bibliography

Sources and Research

All primary and secondary sources used in this project, cited in MLA format with annotations explaining how each source contributed to the argument.

A Note on Sources

All sources were selected for credibility and direct relevance to the thesis. Primary sources come from the National Archives (including the Nixon Presidential Library), the Miller Center at the University of Virginia, congressional records, and the U.S. Supreme Court. Secondary sources are peer-reviewed scholarship or award-winning journalism. No factual claim in this project relies on Wikipedia as a primary reference. Every date, vote count, and polling figure was verified through an institutional or peer-reviewed source.

Primary Sources 4 sources

Primary Source

"Richard Nixon White House Tapes, 'Smoking Gun' Tape Transcript, June 23, 1972." Nixon Presidential Library and Museum, National Archives and Records Administration, College Park, MD. National Archives, www.archives.gov/research/investigations/watergate/nixon-tapes.

This tape, in which Nixon orders the CIA to block the FBI's Watergate investigation, is the most important single document in the entire scandal. It proved Nixon's direct involvement in obstruction of justice just six days after the break-in, and its release in August 1974 immediately ended his presidency. I used it in the timeline, the Multiple Perspectives page, and the Revolution section as the core evidence for the thesis.

Primary Source

"The Articles of Impeachment Adopted by the House Committee on the Judiciary." United States House of Representatives, Committee on the Judiciary, 93rd Congress, 2nd Session. July 27-30, 1974. National Archives, www.archives.gov.

The three Articles of Impeachment, covering obstruction of justice, abuse of power, and contempt of Congress, are the official legal summary of what Nixon did and why it was impeachable. I used them throughout the site, especially in the Revolution section and the scandal timeline, to categorize Nixon's specific abuses of power.

Primary Source

United States Supreme Court. "United States v. Nixon." 418 U.S. 683. Decided July 24, 1974. Opinion by Chief Justice Warren E. Burger. Available through Justia U.S. Supreme Court and the Library of Congress.

The unanimous 8 to 0 ruling established that executive privilege cannot shield criminal evidence from a legitimate prosecution. It is the most constitutionally significant legal document in Watergate and shaped the Reaction section, the timeline entry for July 24, 1974, and the Significance page's discussion of lasting precedents. The fact that Nixon's own appointees voted against him made the ruling unanswerable.

Primary Source

Nixon, Richard M. "Address to the Nation Announcing Decision to Resign the Presidency." The White House, Washington, D.C., August 8, 1974. Miller Center, University of Virginia, millercenter.org/the-presidency/presidential-speeches/august-8-1974-address-nation-announcing-decision-resign.

Nixon's resignation speech is analyzed for what it omits: he frames his departure as a political sacrifice and never admits guilt or wrongdoing. I used it in the Multiple Perspectives section on Nixon's perspective and in the timeline to illustrate the gap between his self-understanding and the historical reality that the tapes had established.

Secondary Sources 4 sources

Secondary Source

Kutler, Stanley I. "The Wars of Watergate: The Last Crisis of Richard Nixon." Alfred A. Knopf, 1990.

Kutler's 614-page scholarly history is the most thorough single-volume treatment of Watergate written by a professional historian. I drew on it throughout the project, especially for the Imperial Presidency analysis in Historical Context, the reform analysis in the RRR page, and the public trust data and legislative reform analysis in Significance and Legacy.

Secondary Source

Schlesinger, Arthur M., Jr. "The Imperial Presidency." Houghton Mifflin, 1973.

Schlesinger coined the term "imperial presidency" and showed that the concentration of executive power Nixon exploited had been building for decades before him. This was essential for contextualizing the Revolution section and the Historical Context page: Nixon did not create the conditions that made Watergate possible, he just took them further than any predecessor.

Secondary Source

Woodward, Bob, and Carl Bernstein. "All the President's Men." Simon and Schuster, 1974.

Woodward and Bernstein's account of how they investigated and broke the Watergate story functions as both a primary source (documenting the investigation's methodology) and a secondary source (analyzing how investigative journalism works in a democracy). I used it in the Multiple Perspectives press section and in the Significance page's discussion of how Watergate transformed political journalism.

Secondary Source

Miller Center, University of Virginia. "Watergate and the Resignation of Richard Nixon." Miller Center, millercenter.org/the-presidency/presidential-history/watergate-and-resignation-richard-nixon. Accessed May 2026.

The Miller Center's Watergate resources, including presidential speech transcripts, historical essays by presidential scholars, and the Nixon recordings archive, were used throughout the project. Their analysis was especially valuable for the Historical Context page's treatment of Nixon's political career and the Imperial Presidency concept. The Miller Center is one of the most authoritative sources on American presidential history.

This project was built primarily on primary source research: the White House tapes, congressional records, Supreme Court opinions, and Nixon's own public statements, supplemented by peer-reviewed scholarship. Every factual claim was verified through at least one institutional or peer-reviewed source. Where primary sources were ambiguous or contested, I said so explicitly rather than ignoring the complexity, as in the Multiple Perspectives treatment of Nixon's defenders.

Annotated Bibliography

Sources & Research

All primary and secondary sources used in this project, cited in MLA format with annotations explaining how each source contributed to the argument.

A Note on Sources

All sources were selected for credibility and direct relevance to the thesis. Primary sources come from the National Archives (including the Nixon Presidential Library), the Miller Center at the University of Virginia, congressional records, and the U.S. Supreme Court. Secondary sources are peer-reviewed scholarship or award-winning journalism. No factual claim in this project relies on Wikipedia — every date, vote count, and polling figure was verified through an institutional or peer-reviewed source.

Primary Sources 8 sources

Primary Source

"The Articles of Impeachment Adopted by the House Committee on the Judiciary." United States House of Representatives, Committee on the Judiciary, 93rd Congress, 2nd Session. July 27–30, 1974. Archived by the National Archives and Records Administration. National Archives, www.archives.gov.

The three Articles of Impeachment — covering obstruction of justice, abuse of power, and contempt of Congress — are the clearest official statement of what Nixon did and why it was impeachable. I used this source in the "Revolution" section of the RRR page and in the scandal timeline to categorize Nixon's abuses of power.

Primary Source

Dean, John W. Testimony Before the Senate Select Committee on Presidential Campaign Activities. United States Senate, 93rd Congress, 1st Session. June 25–29, 1973. Archived in the Records of the U.S. Senate, National Archives, Record Group 46.

Dean's five days of Senate testimony provided the most detailed inside account of the cover-up available before the tapes were released. I used it in the whistleblowers section of the Multiple Perspectives page and in the scandal timeline. What makes it especially valuable as a primary source is that the tapes later confirmed it in nearly every detail.

Primary Source

Nixon, Richard M. "Address to the Nation Announcing Decision to Resign the Presidency." The White House, Washington, D.C., August 8, 1974. Miller Center, University of Virginia, millercenter.org/the-presidency/presidential-speeches/august-8-1974-address-nation-announcing-decision-resign.

Nixon's resignation speech is significant not just for what it says but for what it omits: he frames his departure as a political sacrifice, never admitting guilt. I analyzed this in the Multiple Perspectives page's Nixon section as evidence of the gap between his self-understanding and the historical reality the tapes had established.

Primary Source

"Richard Nixon White House Tapes — 'Smoking Gun' Tape Transcript, June 23, 1972." Nixon Presidential Library and Museum, National Archives and Records Administration, College Park, MD. Transcript available at National Archives, www.archives.gov/research/investigations/watergate/nixon-tapes.

The June 23, 1972 tape — in which Nixon directs the CIA to block the FBI's Watergate investigation — is the single most important document in this project. It proved Nixon's personal involvement in obstruction of justice just six days after the break-in, and ended his presidency when released on August 5, 1974. I used it in the scandal timeline, the Multiple Perspectives page, and the RRR "Revolution" section as the core documentary evidence of the abuse this project analyzes.

Primary Source

Nixon, Richard M. Press Conference, Associated Press Managing Editors Meeting, Walt Disney World, Orlando, Florida. November 17, 1973. Transcript in the Public Papers of the Presidents of the United States: Richard Nixon, 1973. U.S. Government Printing Office, 1975. Also available through the Miller Center, University of Virginia.

This press conference — where Nixon said "I am not a crook" — illustrates the gap between his public denials and the private reality on the tapes. I used it in the scandal timeline and the Multiple Perspectives page to show how Nixon tried to frame the scandal for public consumption right up until the evidence became undeniable.

Primary Source

Select Committee on Presidential Campaign Activities, United States Senate. Final Report of the Senate Select Committee on Presidential Campaign Activities. 93rd Congress, 2nd Session, Senate Report 93-981. U.S. Government Printing Office, 1974.

The Senate Watergate Committee's 1,250-page final report is one of the most comprehensive official accounts of the scandal. I used it throughout the project, especially in the scandal timeline and the RRR "Reaction" section. Its bipartisan authorship — Republicans and Democrats together — makes it strong evidence that the congressional reaction to Nixon wasn't purely partisan.

Primary Source

United States Supreme Court. United States v. Nixon. 418 U.S. 683. Decided July 24, 1974. Opinion by Chief Justice Warren E. Burger. Available through Justia U.S. Supreme Court and the Library of Congress.

The unanimous ruling in U.S. v. Nixon — holding that executive privilege cannot shield criminal evidence from prosecution — is the most constitutionally significant legal document in the whole story. I analyzed it in the scandal timeline, the Multiple Perspectives judiciary section, and the RRR "Reaction" section. The 8–0 vote, including Nixon's four appointees, made it constitutionally unanswerable.

Primary Source

McCord, James W. Letter to Chief Judge John J. Sirica, U.S. District Court for the District of Columbia. March 19, 1973. Read into the court record March 23, 1973. Archived in the Nixon Presidential Library and Museum, National Archives. Reproduced in the Congressional Record and multiple archival collections.

McCord's letter to Sirica — revealing political pressure to stay silent and that perjury had been committed — was the first major crack in the cover-up. I used it in the scandal timeline to show how Judge Sirica's aggressive judicial strategy forced truth-telling, and in the RRR "Reaction" section as an example of the cover-up breaking from an unexpected direction.

Secondary Sources 6 sources

Secondary Source

Kutler, Stanley I. The Wars of Watergate: The Last Crisis of Richard Nixon. Alfred A. Knopf, 1990.

Kutler's 614-page scholarly history is the most thorough single-volume treatment of Watergate written by a professional historian. I used it throughout the project — especially for the "Imperial Presidency" analysis in Historical Context, the reform analysis in the RRR page, and the public trust data and legacy analysis in Significance. It's the backbone of the secondary source research for this project.

Secondary Source

Miller Center, University of Virginia. "Watergate and the Resignation of Richard Nixon." Miller Center, millercenter.org/the-presidency/presidential-history/watergate-and-resignation-richard-nixon. Accessed May 2026.

The Miller Center's Watergate resources — speech transcripts, historical essays by presidential scholars, and the Nixon recordings archive — were used throughout the project. Their analysis was especially valuable for the Historical Context page's treatment of Nixon's political career and the "Imperial Presidency" concept. The Miller Center is one of the most rigorous and authoritative sources on American presidential history.

Secondary Source

PBS American Experience. "Nixon." Produced by David Grubin. PBS, 1990. Transcript archived at PBS American Experience, pbs.org/wgbh/americanexperience/films/nixon.

This documentary features interviews with dozens of participants and observers, making it especially useful for understanding Nixon's psychology and the human dimensions of the crisis. I used it primarily for the Historical Context biographical section and the Multiple Perspectives analysis of Nixon's perspective. The firsthand accounts from White House aides and congressional investigators supplemented the documentary record well.

Secondary Source

Schlesinger, Arthur M., Jr. The Imperial Presidency. Houghton Mifflin, 1973.

Schlesinger coined the term "imperial presidency" and shows that the concentration of executive power Nixon exploited had been building for decades. I used this in the Historical Context "Imperial Presidency" section and in the RRR "Revolution" section to establish that Nixon's abuses were an extreme extension of existing trends, not something he created from scratch. That distinction matters for understanding the thesis.

Secondary Source

Woodward, Bob, and Carl Bernstein. All the President's Men. Simon & Schuster, 1974.

Woodward and Bernstein's account of how they broke the story is both a primary source (documenting the investigation itself) and a secondary source (analyzing how investigative journalism functions in a democracy). I used it in the Multiple Perspectives press section and in the Significance page's discussion of how Watergate transformed investigative journalism. Their documentation of the two-source rule and editorial pressure from Bradlee shows what responsible accountability journalism actually looks like.

Secondary Source

Lukas, J. Anthony. Nightmare: The Underside of the Nixon Years. Viking Press, 1976.

Lukas's Pulitzer Prize-winning account covers the full range of Nixon-era abuses — the Huston Plan, the Plumbers, the enemies list, the IRS harassment campaign — beyond just the Watergate break-in. I used it in Historical Context, the Multiple Perspectives analysis of Nixon's defenders, and the RRR "Revolution" section's enumeration of Nixon's specific abuses. It's the most comprehensive single source for understanding the breadth of what went wrong.

Research Process

I built this project primarily on primary sources — the White House tapes, congressional records, Supreme Court opinions, and Nixon's own public statements — supplemented by peer-reviewed scholarship. Every factual claim was verified through at least one institutional or peer-reviewed source. I used Wikipedia occasionally to find primary sources and cross-check dates, but not as a primary reference for any factual claim.

Where primary sources were ambiguous or contested, I said so explicitly rather than papering over the dispute — for example, in the Multiple Perspectives treatment of Nixon's defenders, where the "everyone does it" argument is taken seriously before being answered. The goal was to be honest about complexity while still making a clear historical argument.

Historical Context

The World That Made Watergate Possible

Watergate didn't come out of nowhere. The Cold War, Vietnam, and two decades of expanding presidential power all set the stage for what Nixon did — and help explain why he thought he could get away with it.

Thesis Connection

The "revolutionary" abuse at the core of Watergate was only possible because of the conditions that existed in 1972 — a Cold War culture of executive secrecy, a public exhausted by Vietnam, and a presidency that had been accumulating unchecked power for decades. This page explains those conditions.

The Big Picture

Cold War America and the Cult of Secrecy

To understand how Nixon could justify bugging political opponents, breaking into a psychiatrist's office, and directing the CIA to obstruct an FBI investigation, you have to understand the world he was operating in. The Cold War was more than a foreign policy standoff — it was a mindset that had justified decades of executive secrecy in the name of national security.

Since Truman, presidents had made war, run covert operations, and expanded their authority — often without telling Congress — because "fighting communism" seemed to override the normal rules. By the time Nixon took office in 1969, the presidency had accumulated enormous unilateral power, and the national security apparatus had a deep institutional reflex for doing things in secret. Nixon didn't create that culture. He just took it further than anyone had before.

Nixon's team believed that antiwar protesters, leaking journalists, and Democratic rivals were genuine threats — not just political opponents but national security risks. That thinking made it easy to justify things that were clearly illegal. The White House "Plumbers" unit, set up in 1971 to stop leaks, was a direct product of that mindset. The Plumbers eventually recruited the Watergate burglars.

Vietnam

The War That Broke Public Trust

[Photo: Antiwar protesters march in Washington D.C., 1971. The massive May Day protests drew hundreds of thousands and deepened Nixon's sense that his political enemies were everywhere.]

Vietnam War protests in Washington D.C., 1971

Credit: Library of Congress, Prints and Photographs Division

Vietnam is the most important piece of context for Watergate. By 1969, when Nixon inherited the war, more than 36,000 Americans had died and public opinion had already turned. Kent State (May 4, 1970) — when National Guard troops killed four student protesters at Ohio State — became a symbol of how completely the country had fractured.

Nixon responded to antiwar dissent not with compromise but with surveillance. The "Huston Plan" of 1970 authorized illegal wiretapping, mail opening, and break-ins against domestic dissidents. Hoover refused to fully implement it, but it showed exactly how the Nixon White House thought about its critics: as enemies to be monitored and suppressed, not citizens to be listened to.

The Pentagon Papers crisis in June 1971 made everything worse. When Daniel Ellsberg leaked classified Defense Department documents showing how the government had misled the public about Vietnam, Nixon went after Ellsberg criminally and created the Plumbers to plug leaks — the same unit that would plan the Watergate break-in. The line from Vietnam to Watergate is direct.

Vietnam also matters because it had already destroyed public trust in government before Watergate even happened. A generation of Americans had watched their leaders lie to them about the war for years. Watergate hit a country that was already deeply skeptical — and made things dramatically worse.

The Man

Nixon: A Political Survivor Who Never Stopped Feeling Threatened

Richard Nixon's whole political identity was built on coming back from the brink. He rose to national fame in the late 1940s by pursuing Alger Hiss for espionage. He survived a campaign fund scandal in 1952 with his famous "Checkers" TV speech. He lost the 1960 presidential race to Kennedy by a tiny margin, then lost the California governor's race in 1962 and announced he was done with politics. Then he came back and won the presidency in 1968.

That pattern of near-defeat and comeback shaped how Nixon saw the world. He kept an actual "enemies list" of journalists, politicians, and celebrities he wanted harassed through IRS audits and other government tools. This wasn't just ordinary political hardball — it was a systematic, organized effort to use federal power against private citizens. Nixon was deeply paranoid, and that paranoia made the Watergate cover-up feel necessary to him even when it clearly wasn't.

Nixon's Political Timeline

  • 1946: Elected to Congress on aggressive anti-communist platform
  • 1948: Pursues Alger Hiss case, gains national recognition
  • 1952: Eisenhower's VP; survives fund scandal with "Checkers" speech
  • 1960: Loses presidency to Kennedy by 0.17% of the popular vote
  • 1962: Loses California governor's race; calls it quits
  • 1968: Returns and wins the White House in a three-way race
  • 1972: Wins 49-state landslide re-election
  • 1974: Resigns August 9 — the only president ever to do so
The Structure

The "Imperial Presidency" — Power Without Accountability

This is where "Revolution" begins

Historian Arthur Schlesinger Jr. coined the term "imperial presidency" in his 1973 book of the same name. His argument was that Cold War presidents had been quietly accumulating power that the Constitution had assigned to Congress — especially the power to make war — for decades. By 1972, the presidency could conduct secret bombing campaigns, run global intelligence operations, and spend or impound congressional money largely on its own judgment.

Nixon took all of this and pushed it to its logical extreme. He impounded funds Congress had appropriated. He secretly bombed Cambodia without informing Congress. He claimed executive privilege over virtually anything he wanted to hide. And when the Watergate break-in created a legal threat, he turned the entire apparatus of the executive branch toward a cover-up. That's the revolutionary abuse this project is about — not just ordinary corruption, but a president trying to place himself permanently above the law.

The Election

1972: A Landslide Built on Paranoia

On November 7, 1972, Nixon won re-election by one of the largest margins in American history — 49 of 50 states, 520 electoral votes, 60.7% of the popular vote. His genuine achievements were real: the opening of China, the SALT arms treaty, the beginning of détente with the Soviet Union. He could have won easily.

But Nixon also ran a massive sabotage campaign through his Committee to Re-Elect the President (CREEP), financing break-ins, forged letters, and espionage against Democratic campaigns. That's the central irony of Watergate: the crimes that ended his presidency were completely unnecessary. He was going to win anyway. His paranoia wouldn't let him trust that.

My Take: Context Explains But Doesn't Excuse

When I look at all this context together, I think the most important thing to understand is that Nixon didn't invent the conditions that made Watergate possible. The Cold War culture of secrecy, the expanded executive power, the domestic surveillance programs — those had been building for decades before Nixon got to the White House.

But other presidents operated in the same environment without compiling enemies lists, directing campaign sabotage, or ordering obstruction of justice. What made Nixon different was that he turned all of that existing power toward the specific goal of keeping himself in power by any means necessary. That's what makes Watergate a genuine revolution in the abuse of presidential authority — not just another instance of ordinary political corruption.

The Scandal Unfolds

From Break-In to Resignation

Twenty-six months. That is how long it took from a taped-open door at the Watergate complex to Richard Nixon's helicopter lifting off from the White House lawn. Click each event below to explore the details — and understand how a "third-rate burglary" unraveled a presidency.

Thesis Connection

Every event on this timeline is part of a single arc: the revolutionary abuse of power Nixon initiated with the cover-up, the reaction of investigators, journalists, and courts who dismantled it piece by piece, and the reforms that followed. Watch for how each escalation by Nixon produced a corresponding — and ultimately larger — reaction that he could not control.

The Watergate Chronology

June 17, 1972
The Watergate Break-In
The Break-In

At approximately 2:30 a.m., security guard Frank Wills noticed tape covering the latch of a door in the Watergate office complex — the kind of tape used by burglars to keep a door from locking. He removed it; someone replaced it. He called the police. Officers arrested five men inside the Democratic National Committee headquarters, all wearing surgical gloves and carrying sophisticated surveillance equipment, thousands of dollars in cash, and documents linked to the Committee to Re-Elect the President (CREEP).

The five men — Bernard Barker, Virgilio Gonzalez, Eugenio Martinez, Frank Sturgis, and James McCord (CREEP's security director) — were not ordinary burglars. Several had CIA connections and had participated in the Bay of Pigs operation. Their presence inside the DNC headquarters on the night of June 17 was not a random crime. It was the final act of a systematic program of political espionage and sabotage organized by CREEP operatives G. Gordon Liddy and E. Howard Hunt, approved by senior White House officials.

Thesis connection: The break-in itself, while serious, was merely the visible tip of the revolutionary abuse of power. The real scandal lay in what Nixon did next.

June 20, 1972
Nixon and Haldeman Discuss the Break-In (18.5-Minute Gap)
The Break-In

Nixon met with Chief of Staff H.R. Haldeman for almost two hours to discuss the break-in. The White House taping system recorded the conversation — but when the tape was later subpoenaed, an 18.5-minute portion had been erased. Nixon's personal secretary, Rose Mary Woods, claimed she had accidentally erased it while transcribing. Independent analysis determined that the gap required multiple deliberate erasures and could not have been accidental. The 18.5-minute gap became one of the most infamous silences in American history.

June 23, 1972
The "Smoking Gun" — Nixon Orders CIA to Block FBI Investigation
The Cover-Up

This is the single most damaging moment in the entire Watergate scandal — the moment that made Nixon's guilt undeniable. On this date, Nixon and Haldeman discussed using the CIA to tell the FBI that the Watergate investigation was "a CIA thing" and to stop their inquiry. The tape recording of this conversation — preserved by the White House's own secret taping system — is the "smoking gun" that ended Nixon's presidency when it was finally released in August 1974.

On the tape, Nixon is explicit: "Call the CIA ... Don't go any further into this case, period!" This was obstruction of justice. This was the president of the United States ordering the intelligence agencies of the United States to obstruct a legitimate criminal investigation into his own campaign's crimes. When this tape was finally released by court order in August 1974, even Nixon's most loyal Republican defenders in Congress announced they could no longer support him.

Primary source: Transcript of White House tape, June 23, 1972 — available from the National Archives, Nixon Presidential Library, College Park, MD.

August 29, 1972
Nixon Claims White House Investigation Found "No Wrongdoing"
The Cover-Up

At a press conference, Nixon declared that White House Counsel John Dean had conducted a thorough investigation and found that no one in the White House had been involved in the Watergate break-in. This statement was false. Dean had conducted no such investigation, and Nixon had known about administration involvement almost from the first day. This was Nixon's first major public lie about Watergate — and it established the pattern of deception he would maintain for the next two years.

November 7, 1972
Nixon Wins 49-State Landslide Re-Election
The Cover-Up

Nixon wins one of the largest electoral landslides in American history — 520 electoral votes to McGovern's 17. The ongoing Watergate investigation, still largely confined to newspaper stories, has no significant impact on the election. Nixon's 60.7% popular vote victory demonstrates the central irony of Watergate: he never needed the crimes that destroyed him. The cover-up was unnecessary. Yet the machinery of deception was already set in motion, and Nixon's re-election did nothing to slow it — if anything, it convinced his team that they were untouchable.

January–March 1973
The Watergate Burglars' Trial — Sirica Applies Pressure
The Cover-Up

The five Watergate burglars, plus Hunt and Liddy, were tried before Chief Judge John Sirica in U.S. District Court. All pleaded guilty or were convicted. Sirica, sensing that the full story had not come out, imposed severe provisional sentences — McCord received up to 40 years — and invited the defendants to cooperate further. Sirica's aggressive judicial approach was a crucial catalyst. On March 23, 1973, he read a letter from McCord in open court: McCord revealed that the defendants had been under "political pressure to plead guilty and remain silent," that perjury had been committed, and that higher-ups in the administration were involved. The cover-up was beginning to crack.

March 21, 1973
"A Cancer on the Presidency" — Dean's Warning
The Cover-Up

White House Counsel John Dean met with Nixon in the Oval Office and delivered a stark warning: "We have a cancer within — close to the presidency — that is growing. It is growing daily." Dean laid out the entire cover-up in explicit detail, explaining that hush money was being paid to the Watergate defendants and that the demands would only grow. He estimated it would take a million dollars to keep the conspirators quiet.

Nixon's response, captured on tape, was not to end the cover-up. It was to figure out how to sustain it. "We could get that," Nixon replied about the million dollars. This meeting is one of the most damning pieces of evidence that Nixon not only knew about the cover-up but actively directed it. Dean, recognizing that he was being positioned to take the fall, began cooperating with federal prosecutors shortly thereafter.

April 30, 1973
Haldeman and Ehrlichman Resign; Dean Is Fired
The Cover-Up

In a nationally televised address, Nixon announced the resignations of his two closest aides — Chief of Staff H.R. Haldeman and Domestic Policy Advisor John Ehrlichman — and the firing of John Dean. Nixon called them "two of the finest public servants it has been my privilege to know," which was widely understood as Nixon sacrificing them to save himself. Attorney General Richard Kleindienst also resigned. Nixon appointed Elliot Richardson as the new Attorney General and agreed to allow Richardson to appoint an independent special prosecutor to investigate Watergate.

May 17, 1973
Senate Watergate Committee Hearings Begin
Investigation & Reaction

The Senate Select Committee on Presidential Campaign Activities — chaired by Senator Sam Ervin of North Carolina, a soft-spoken constitutional scholar who became famous for quoting scripture and the Constitution with equal facility — began nationally televised hearings. For months, millions of Americans watched live as witness after witness described the criminal conspiracy that had operated out of the Nixon White House. The hearings were a turning point in public opinion: Americans who had been willing to give the president the benefit of the doubt began, in huge numbers, to conclude that something deeply wrong had occurred.

Republican Senator Howard Baker's famous question — "What did the president know, and when did he know it?" — became the defining inquiry of the entire crisis.

June 25–29, 1973
John Dean Testifies Before the Senate Watergate Committee
Investigation & Reaction

In five days of riveting televised testimony, Nixon's former counsel — reading from a 245-page prepared statement — described in precise detail how the cover-up worked, who was involved, and how Nixon himself had directed it. Dean placed the president at the center of the conspiracy. He described the March 21, 1973 "cancer on the presidency" meeting in damning detail. He revealed the enemies list. He testified about hush money payments to the Watergate defendants.

Dean's testimony was explosive, but it was his word against Nixon's. The question of who to believe remained unanswered — until three weeks later, when a minor White House aide named Alexander Butterfield changed everything.

July 16, 1973
Alexander Butterfield Reveals the White House Taping System
Investigation & Reaction

In what may be the single most consequential revelation of the entire Watergate scandal, former White House aide Alexander Butterfield told Senate investigators — and then testified publicly before the Ervin Committee — that Nixon had installed a voice-activated secret taping system in the White House in 1971. Every conversation in the Oval Office, the Cabinet Room, the Lincoln sitting room, and the EOB office had been automatically recorded.

The existence of the tapes transformed the investigation entirely. Dean had said X. Nixon said Dean was lying. The tapes would settle the question. Both the Senate Watergate Committee and Special Prosecutor Archibald Cox immediately subpoenaed the tapes. Nixon refused to hand them over. The battle over the tapes — fought through courts, a constitutional crisis, and ultimately a unanimous Supreme Court ruling — was the final phase of Watergate.

October 20, 1973
The Saturday Night Massacre
Investigation & Reaction

This is the moment Watergate became a constitutional crisis in the eyes of most Americans. Special Prosecutor Archibald Cox, a Harvard Law professor appointed to investigate Watergate, refused to accept Nixon's offer of edited transcripts of the subpoenaed tapes. Nixon ordered Attorney General Elliot Richardson to fire Cox. Richardson refused and resigned. Nixon ordered Deputy Attorney General William Ruckelshaus to fire Cox. Ruckelshaus refused and was himself fired. Solicitor General Robert Bork, third in the Department of Justice hierarchy, finally carried out the order.

The reaction was immediate and overwhelming. Within 48 hours, more than 300,000 telegrams flooded Capitol Hill — the largest volume in congressional history. Impeachment resolutions were introduced in Congress. Nixon's approval rating dropped to 27% — a record low at the time. One of his own Republican allies reportedly called the reaction "a firestorm." The Saturday Night Massacre did not just expose Nixon's guilt; it revealed that he was willing to obstruct justice openly, in full public view. That revelation made the reaction against him irreversible.

November 17, 1973
"I Am Not a Crook" — Nixon's Press Conference
Investigation & Reaction

Under intense public and media pressure, Nixon held a televised press conference before 400 Associated Press managing editors in Orlando, Florida. During a question-and-answer session, Nixon delivered what became one of the most notorious statements in presidential history: "I am not a crook. I've earned everything I've got." The statement — defensive, unconvincing, and politically damaging — became emblematic of Nixon's inability to manage the growing crisis. The phrase entered American political vocabulary as a symbol of political dishonesty.

February 6, 1974
House Authorizes Judiciary Committee Impeachment Inquiry
Investigation & Reaction

The House of Representatives voted 410–4 to authorize the House Judiciary Committee, chaired by Representative Peter Rodino of New Jersey, to investigate whether sufficient grounds existed to impeach President Nixon. The near-unanimous bipartisan vote signaled that the political cover Nixon had relied upon was gone. The Judiciary Committee began hiring staff, subpoenaing evidence, and conducting the most serious impeachment investigation in American history since the impeachment of Andrew Johnson in 1868.

July 24, 1974
Supreme Court Rules 8–0: Nixon Must Surrender Tapes
Investigation & Reaction

In United States v. Nixon, 418 U.S. 683 (1974), all eight participating justices (William Rehnquist had recused himself) unanimously ruled that Nixon must turn over the subpoenaed White House tapes to Special Prosecutor Leon Jaworski. The Court acknowledged that executive privilege existed but held that it was not absolute — it could not shield evidence of criminal conduct from a legitimate criminal investigation.

Chief Justice Warren Burger, himself a Nixon appointee, wrote the opinion. The unanimity of the ruling was crushing. Nixon had argued that the Court could not compel a president to surrender executive communications. The Court said it could, it had, and Nixon had to comply. This ruling is one of the most important precedents in American constitutional law: it established that the president is subject to judicial process and that no one — not even the president — is above the law.

Primary source: United States v. Nixon, 418 U.S. 683 (1974). Opinion by Chief Justice Warren Burger, July 24, 1974.

July 27–30, 1974
House Judiciary Committee Votes Three Articles of Impeachment
Investigation & Reaction

The House Judiciary Committee voted to recommend three articles of impeachment against Nixon: (1) obstruction of justice in connection with the Watergate investigation; (2) abuse of power by using the CIA, FBI, IRS, and other federal agencies against political opponents; and (3) contempt of Congress for refusing to comply with committee subpoenas. The committee's bipartisan votes — Republicans joined Democrats in voting for Articles I and II — signaled that the full House would almost certainly vote to impeach. Conviction in the Senate, which would require a two-thirds vote, appeared likely as well.

August 5, 1974
The "Smoking Gun" Tape Released — Nixon's Last Support Collapses
Investigation & Reaction

Complying with the Supreme Court's order, Nixon released the transcripts of the June 23, 1972 tape — the "smoking gun." Republican members of Congress who had defended Nixon for two years, including the ten who had voted against all three articles of impeachment in Judiciary Committee, issued statements saying they could no longer support the president. Senator Barry Goldwater, the conservative icon who was Nixon's strongest ideological ally, led a delegation of congressional Republicans to the White House to tell Nixon he had fewer than 15 Senate votes — not nearly enough to survive a conviction vote.

With the tape released, the constitutional and political question was settled. Nixon had ordered obstruction of justice six days after the break-in. He had lied to the public, to Congress, and apparently to his own lawyers about his involvement for more than two years. Resignation was the only remaining option.

August 8, 1974
Nixon Announces His Resignation
Resignation

In a nationally televised address from the Oval Office, Richard Nixon announced that he would resign the presidency effective noon the following day — the first and only American president to resign from office. Nixon did not admit guilt. He said: "I no longer have a strong enough political base in the Congress to justify continuing that effort. ... I have always tried to do what is best for the Nation." He did not apologize for the crimes his administration had committed.

The speech was a masterpiece of evasion — Nixon framed his resignation as a political calculation, not a moral reckoning. But the fact of the resignation was unmistakable: a sitting American president, faced with certain impeachment and likely conviction, had been forced from office by the rule of law and by the institutions — congressional, judicial, and journalistic — that had refused to be silenced.

Primary source: Nixon, Richard M. Resignation Address to the Nation. The White House, August 8, 1974. Available through the Miller Center, University of Virginia, and the National Archives.

August 9, 1974
Gerald Ford Becomes the 38th President
Resignation

At noon on August 9, Nixon's resignation took effect. Gerald Ford, whom Nixon had appointed Vice President under the 25th Amendment after Spiro Agnew's own resignation over corruption charges in October 1973, was sworn in as the 38th President of the United States. Ford's brief inaugural statement — "Our long national nightmare is over" — gave voice to the exhaustion and grief of a nation that had watched its government tear itself apart. Ford inherited a presidency whose credibility was in ruins and whose institutional legitimacy depended on demonstrating that the constitutional system had, in the end, worked.

September 8, 1974
Ford Pardons Nixon — A "Full, Free, and Absolute" Pardon
Reform & Legacy

Less than a month into his presidency, Gerald Ford announced that he was granting Richard Nixon "a full, free, and absolute pardon" for all crimes Nixon "committed or may have committed or taken part in" during his presidency. Ford argued the pardon was necessary to end "the ugly passions" of Watergate and allow the country to move forward. The reaction was immediate and fierce: Ford's press secretary resigned in protest, Ford's approval rating dropped 21 points overnight, and many historians believe the pardon cost Ford the 1976 presidential election.

The pardon meant Nixon would never be tried, never convicted, and never imprisoned. For many Americans, this felt like the final injustice of Watergate — the man most responsible for the constitutional crisis escaped legal accountability entirely. The pardon also ensured that the full story of Nixon's personal culpability was never tested in a court of law.

1974–1978
The Reform Wave — Congress Acts
Reform

In the years following Watergate, Congress passed an extraordinary wave of reform legislation aimed at preventing a recurrence of Nixon's abuses: the Federal Election Campaign Act Amendments (1974) — limiting campaign contributions and establishing public financing of presidential campaigns; the Freedom of Information Act Amendments (1974) — strengthening public access to government records; the Privacy Act (1974) — limiting government data collection on citizens; the Ethics in Government Act (1978) — requiring financial disclosure by senior officials and establishing the independent counsel statute; the Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Act (1978) — creating courts to oversee domestic intelligence gathering; and the Presidential Records Act (1978) — ensuring that White House records belong to the public, not the president.

These reforms collectively represented the most significant restructuring of executive power accountability in American history since the Founding. They were the direct legislative legacy of Watergate's revolutionary abuse and the national reaction it provoked.

Analytical Summary

What the Timeline Reveals

Looking at this timeline as a whole, what strikes me most is the pattern of escalation and miscalculation that runs through Nixon's side of the story. At every stage where the cover-up might have been unwound — when the burglars were arrested, when McCord wrote his letter to Sirica, when Dean warned Nixon about the "cancer" — Nixon chose escalation over disclosure. Each choice deepened his legal jeopardy and made the eventual reckoning worse.

What's equally remarkable is the resilience of the institutions on the other side. The Senate Watergate Committee, Judge Sirica, the House Judiciary Committee, Special Prosecutors Cox and Jaworski, the Washington Post — none of them blinked when Nixon tried to shut them down. The Saturday Night Massacre, which Nixon expected would end the tapes dispute, instead produced the largest congressional mail surge in history and made impeachment a political certainty. The Supreme Court's unanimous ruling in United States v. Nixon — all eight justices, four of them Nixon's own appointees — demonstrated that the constitutional system of checks and balances was stronger than one man's determination to evade accountability.

That is the essential story of Watergate as it relates to our NHD theme: a revolutionary abuse of power met a reaction powerful enough to overcome it, and the result was a set of reforms designed to ensure it could never happen the same way again. Whether those reforms have been sufficient is a question Americans still debate. But that the reaction happened at all — that the institutions held — is the most important thing to understand about Watergate's place in American history.

The Scandal Unfolds

From Break-In to Resignation

It took 26 months from a taped-open door at the Watergate complex to Nixon's helicopter lifting off the White House lawn. Click any event below to read the details and analysis.

Thesis Connection

Every event on this timeline follows the same arc: Nixon's revolutionary abuse of power, the reaction that dismantled it piece by piece, and the reforms that followed. Notice how each escalation by Nixon produced a bigger reaction that he couldn't control.

The Watergate Chronology

June 17, 1972
The Watergate Break-In
The Break-In

At 2:30 a.m., security guard Frank Wills noticed tape on a door latch at the Watergate complex. He called the police. Officers arrested five men inside the Democratic National Committee headquarters — all wearing surgical gloves, carrying surveillance equipment and cash, and carrying documents linking them to Nixon's Committee to Re-Elect the President (CREEP).

The burglars — Bernard Barker, Virgilio Gonzalez, Eugenio Martinez, Frank Sturgis, and CREEP security director James McCord — weren't random criminals. Several had CIA connections. The break-in was organized by CREEP operatives G. Gordon Liddy and E. Howard Hunt. But the break-in itself was only the beginning. The cover-up is where Nixon became directly involved.

June 20, 1972
Nixon and Haldeman Meet — the 18.5-Minute Gap
The Break-In

Three days after the break-in, Nixon met with Chief of Staff H.R. Haldeman to discuss it. The White House's own taping system recorded the conversation — but when the tape was later subpoenaed, an 18.5-minute section had been erased. Nixon's secretary Rose Mary Woods claimed she accidentally erased it. Independent experts concluded it required multiple deliberate erasures. The 18.5-minute gap is one of the most famous silences in American political history.

June 23, 1972
The "Smoking Gun" Tape — Nixon Orders the CIA to Block the FBI
The Cover-Up

This is the most damaging moment in the entire scandal. Six days after the break-in, Nixon told Haldeman to have the CIA tell the FBI that the Watergate investigation was "a CIA thing" and to stop it. On tape, Nixon is explicit: "Call the CIA... Don't go any further into this case, period!" That is obstruction of justice — a federal crime. When this tape was finally released in August 1974, every remaining Republican defender of Nixon in Congress immediately abandoned him.

Primary source: Transcript of White House tape, June 23, 1972, Nixon Presidential Library, National Archives.

August 29, 1972
Nixon Claims the White House Found "No Wrongdoing"
The Cover-Up

At a press conference, Nixon announced that White House Counsel John Dean had conducted a thorough investigation and found no White House involvement in Watergate. This was false — Dean had conducted no such investigation, and Nixon had known about the cover-up almost immediately. This was Nixon's first major public lie about Watergate and established the pattern he would maintain for the next two years.

November 7, 1972
Nixon Wins 49-State Landslide
The Cover-Up

Nixon wins re-election in a massive landslide — 520 electoral votes, 60.7% popular vote. The Watergate investigation, still mostly confined to newspaper stories, has no significant impact on the result. This is the central irony of the whole story: Nixon didn't need to cheat. He was going to win by enormous margins regardless. The crimes that destroyed his presidency were completely unnecessary.

March 21, 1973
"A Cancer on the Presidency" — Dean's Warning
The Cover-Up

White House Counsel John Dean met with Nixon and delivered a stark warning: "We have a cancer within — close to the presidency — that is growing daily." Dean laid out the entire cover-up in explicit detail, explaining that hush money was being paid to defendants and that it would eventually take a million dollars to keep everyone quiet. Nixon's response, captured on tape, was to figure out how to sustain the cover-up — not end it. "We could get that," he said about the million dollars. This meeting is one of the most damning pieces of evidence against Nixon.

April 30, 1973
Haldeman and Ehrlichman Resign; Dean Is Fired
The Cover-Up

Nixon announced in a televised address that his two closest aides — Chief of Staff H.R. Haldeman and Domestic Policy Advisor John Ehrlichman — were resigning, and that John Dean had been fired. Nixon described them as "two of the finest public servants" — a clear attempt to give them a dignified exit while distancing himself. Attorney General Kleindienst also resigned. Elliot Richardson was appointed as the new AG and agreed to appoint an independent special prosecutor.

May 17, 1973
Senate Watergate Committee Hearings Begin
Investigation

The Senate Select Committee on Presidential Campaign Activities — chaired by Senator Sam Ervin of North Carolina — began nationally televised hearings. For months, millions of Americans watched live as witness after witness described a criminal conspiracy run out of the Nixon White House. Republican Senator Howard Baker's question became the defining line of the entire crisis: "What did the president know, and when did he know it?"

June 25–29, 1973
John Dean Testifies Before the Senate
Investigation

Nixon's former lawyer spent five days reading from a 245-page prepared statement, placing Nixon at the center of the cover-up in precise detail. He described the March 21 "cancer" conversation, the hush money payments, and the enemies list. Dean's testimony was explosive, but at the time it was his word against Nixon's. That changed three weeks later when a minor White House aide changed everything.

July 16, 1973
Alexander Butterfield Reveals the White House Taping System
Investigation

This is probably the single most consequential moment of the entire scandal. Former White House aide Alexander Butterfield told Senate investigators — and then testified publicly — that Nixon had installed a secret voice-activated taping system in 1971. Every conversation in the Oval Office had been recorded automatically. The Senate committee and Special Prosecutor Archibald Cox immediately subpoenaed the tapes. Nixon refused to hand them over. The battle over the tapes became the final phase of Watergate.

October 20, 1973
The Saturday Night Massacre
Investigation

This is when Watergate became a constitutional crisis. Special Prosecutor Archibald Cox refused to accept Nixon's offer of edited tape transcripts. Nixon ordered Attorney General Elliot Richardson to fire Cox. Richardson refused and resigned. Nixon ordered Deputy AG William Ruckelshaus to fire Cox. Ruckelshaus refused and was fired. Solicitor General Robert Bork finally carried out the order.

The reaction was immediate. Within 48 hours, more than 300,000 telegrams flooded Capitol Hill — the largest in congressional history. Impeachment resolutions were introduced from both parties. Nixon's approval rating dropped to 27%. The Saturday Night Massacre didn't just expose Nixon's guilt — it proved he was willing to obstruct justice openly, in public. That made the reaction against him irreversible.

November 17, 1973
"I Am Not a Crook"
Investigation

At a press conference before 400 Associated Press editors, Nixon delivered one of the most famous and most damaging statements in presidential history: "I am not a crook. I've earned everything I've got." The statement — defensive, unconvincing, and politically tone-deaf — became a symbol of his inability to manage the crisis. The phrase entered American political vocabulary as shorthand for political dishonesty.

July 24, 1974
Supreme Court Rules 8–0: Nixon Must Hand Over the Tapes
Investigation

In United States v. Nixon, 418 U.S. 683 (1974), all eight participating justices unanimously ruled that Nixon had to turn over the subpoenaed tapes. The Court acknowledged that executive privilege existed but held it wasn't absolute — it couldn't shield evidence of criminal conduct from a legitimate criminal investigation. Chief Justice Warren Burger, a Nixon appointee, wrote the opinion. The 8–0 vote was deliberate: the Court wanted no room for Nixon to maneuver.

This ruling is one of the most important precedents in American constitutional law. It established that no president is above the law or exempt from producing evidence in a criminal proceeding.

July 27–30, 1974
House Judiciary Committee Votes Three Articles of Impeachment
Investigation

Chaired by Representative Peter Rodino of New Jersey, the House Judiciary Committee voted three articles of impeachment: (1) obstruction of justice, (2) abuse of power, and (3) contempt of Congress. The bipartisan votes — Republicans joining Democrats on Articles I and II — signaled that full House impeachment and Senate conviction were likely. Nixon was almost certainly going to be removed from office.

August 5, 1974
The "Smoking Gun" Tape Released — Support Collapses
Resignation

Complying with the Supreme Court order, Nixon released the June 23, 1972 transcript. Every Republican who had defended Nixon in the Judiciary Committee immediately issued statements saying they could no longer support him. Senator Barry Goldwater, the leading conservative icon, led a Republican congressional delegation to the White House to tell Nixon he had fewer than 15 Senate votes — not nearly enough to survive conviction. The political math was over.

August 8, 1974
Nixon Announces His Resignation
Resignation

In a nationally televised address from the Oval Office, Nixon announced he would resign effective the next day — the first and only American president ever to resign. He framed it as a political sacrifice, not a moral reckoning: "I no longer have a strong enough political base in the Congress." He did not admit guilt. He did not apologize. Even at the end, Nixon refused to acknowledge what everyone else had concluded.

Primary source: Nixon, Richard M. Resignation Address to the Nation, August 8, 1974. Miller Center, University of Virginia; National Archives.

August 9, 1974
Ford Becomes President — "Our Long National Nightmare Is Over"
Resignation

Gerald Ford was sworn in as the 38th President. His brief inaugural statement — "Our long national nightmare is over. Our Constitution works" — captured both the exhaustion and the relief the country felt. Ford had been appointed Vice President under the 25th Amendment after Spiro Agnew's own resignation over corruption charges in October 1973, making him the first president in U.S. history elected to neither the presidency nor the vice presidency.

September 8, 1974
Ford Pardons Nixon
Reform & Legacy

Less than a month into his presidency, Ford granted Nixon "a full, free, and absolute pardon" for all offenses committed during his presidency. Ford argued it was necessary to let the country move forward. The backlash was immediate — Ford's press secretary resigned in protest, his approval rating dropped 21 points overnight, and most historians believe the pardon cost Ford the 1976 election. Nixon was never tried, never convicted, and never imprisoned. For many Americans, it felt like the final injustice of Watergate.

1974–1978
Congress Passes the Reform Wave
Reform

In the years after Watergate, Congress passed an extraordinary wave of reform legislation: the Federal Election Campaign Act Amendments (1974), the Freedom of Information Act Amendments (1974), the Ethics in Government Act (1978), the Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Act (1978), the Presidential Records Act (1978), and more. These reforms collectively represented the most significant restructuring of executive accountability since the Founding. They were the direct legislative legacy of everything on this timeline.

Analysis

What the Timeline Shows

Looking at this timeline as a whole, what strikes me most is how every escalation by Nixon made things worse. Each time he tried to shut down the investigation — firing Cox, claiming executive privilege, releasing edited transcripts — the reaction got bigger and harder to contain. The Saturday Night Massacre, which Nixon expected would end the tapes dispute, instead produced 300,000 telegrams to Congress and made impeachment politically inevitable.

What's equally impressive is how the institutions on the other side held. The Senate committee, Judge Sirica, Woodward and Bernstein, Special Prosecutors Cox and Jaworski, and ultimately the Supreme Court (all eight justices, four of them Nixon appointees) — none of them backed down. The constitutional system worked. At enormous cost, with enormous damage to the country's trust in government, but it worked.
